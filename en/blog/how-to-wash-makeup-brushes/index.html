How to Wash Makeup Brushes: A Makeup Artist's Routine

By Nikol Pavlova – Professional Makeup Artist, Prague

Almost nobody loves washing brushes, and makeup artists least of all. But some days I wash fifty or even a hundred of them, so I've built a routine that's fast, cheap and gentle on the bristles. No miracle products and no twenty-minute ritual: a bar of soap, a few rules and the right technique.

The routine in short

  1. Antibacterial bar soap, a silicone mat, gloves and a towel
  2. Always hold a wet brush tip-down, and never soak it
  3. Warm water: not hot, not cold
  4. Round brushes in circles, flat ones in strokes only
  5. Melt foundation and lipstick with a drop of oil first
  6. Wash in batches, say ten at a time
  7. Dry flat on a towel, spaced apart
  8. Away from a humid bathroom, a hot radiator and direct sun

Who it is for

I use this routine on my whole kit: for me it isn't one brush but sometimes fifty or a hundred at once. It works for anyone with a few brushes at home who wants them to last for years, and for new makeup artists too. There's no science to washing brushes, just a few rules worth keeping.

What you'll need

Bar soap
antibacterial and deep-cleansing
Silicone mat
ideally with a ribbed section
Gloves
the same ones you dye your hair in
A flat towel
for drying, even twice as big
Oil
olive or sunflower, for cream textures
Hair conditioner
only for natural bristles that stiffen

The soap has to be a bar, not liquid, and properly cleansing. A gentle creamy soap isn't enough. Laundry soap might work, but it's very alkaline and drying, so I don't recommend it. In the Czech Republic I use Protex; in Ukraine, look for Safeguard labelled Deep Clean.

My silicone mat is from AliExpress and it's on its third year. My favourite part is the ribbed section, which gets out dust and built-up pigment. And the gloves I dye my hair in save my hands when it's dozens of brushes.

Natural or synthetic bristles?

There are only two kinds of brushes: natural and synthetic. There's no third kind, at most a mix, like duo fibre. I used to wash natural brushes with shampoo, because plain soap makes them stiff, and that's true. But experience taught me one thing: a good, pricier brush handles soap just fine.

In a year of using premium brushes from Ukrainian brands I never once needed a mask on them; they never went stiff, whatever I washed them with. Some of the cheap AliExpress brushes I started with, on the other hand, fluff out in half a second, with soap or shampoo. I still use a few of them; the rest sit on the bench.

Pick your brush

Bristles
Quality

Natural · premium

Bar soap

Plain bar soap is fine. If the bristles ever start to stiffen, treat them with hair conditioner or a mask to close the hair cuticle. Briefly, not for hours.

Natural · budget

Shampoo and conditioner

Shampoo instead of soap, then a quick conditioner or hair mask. Don't leave them in it long: just while you finish washing the rest.

Synthetic · premium

Bar soap, no worries

Good synthetics handle soap with no trouble. For cream textures such as foundation or concealer, use a drop of oil first, then soap.

Synthetic · budget

Something gentler

Cheap brushes fluff out quickly with soap. Reach for a gentle shampoo or a dedicated brush cleanser. That's fine; it just costs more.

Why not special cleansers?

Brush soaps and shampoos work; they just aren't as economical as a plain bar. And express cleansers dry the bristles out, so I keep them for when I really need them. Investing in a good brush pays you back one hundred percent.

Three golden rules

The technique is simple, but these rules are non-negotiable. Everything in a brush is held together by glue, and that's what we're protecting.

  1. 1

    Never hold a wet brush bristles-up.

    Water runs into the glue in the ferrule and softens it over time. The bristles start shedding or the brush falls apart.

  2. 2

    Never soak your brushes.

    Don't pile them in the sink or leave them sitting in water. Same reason: the glue.

  3. 3

    Warm water only.

    Cold water won't wash the product out; hot water damages the glue.

Technique by brush shape

Your washing motion should mirror how you use the brush. Pick a shape:

Circles

Round brushes

Wet the brush and move it over the soap bar in circles. Round brush, round motion. Don't press hard, there's no need. Go gently but properly, so the soap works its way inside and lathers.

  1. Lather 10
  2. Rinse 10
  3. Onto the towel

I sort brushes into groups: I lather ten medium or small round ones in a row, rinse all ten, then lay them on the towel.

Strokes and "cat paw"

Flat brushes

Never swirl a flat brush. It's bad for the bristles, because you never work with it in circles, only in strokes. So wash it in strokes too. Tap it into the soap like a cat's paw, stroke back and forth, set it down and let it sit for a moment. The makeup loosens while it rests.

Very gently

Fine liner brushes

Their whole value is in the fine bristles that draw precise lines. Squash them while washing and they fluff out and are hard to get back into shape.

A trick at your own risk

Dip just the bristles, not the plastic, into boiling water for a moment and they pull back together. Some brushes can melt, though.

Circles

Duo fibre

Even though it's a little flattened, you can wash it in circles. That matches its shape and the way you work with it.

Foundation or lipstick that won't wash out

A dense synthetic brush for cream textures with foundation packed deep inside? Or red lipstick? Cleansing oil or micellar water work too, but my most tried-and-tested method is plain kitchen oil.

  1. 01

    A drop of oil

    Olive or sunflower. Really just a tiny drop, and not deep into the brush.

  2. 02

    Dissolve the product

    Work the foundation or lipstick out of the brush with the oil.

  3. 03

    Finish with soap

    Soap removes the oil and any leftovers. Rinse, press, done.

How I found out

By pure accident. I once ran out of soap, only noticed late at night, and had to leave for work with those brushes in the morning. I washed them out with oil and finished with dish soap. Ever since, I always keep a spare bar on hand.

Drying: flat and spaced out

After each batch, lay the brushes on a flat towel. Don't stand them up and don't hang them; there's no need. You can press a flat brush lightly into the towel, but don't squeeze it. Space them out, alternating head-to-toe so the bristles don't touch. They dry overnight.

Where should the towel go?

The bathroom+

NoBathrooms are usually humid. I fold the towel carefully and carry it to my room.

A sill above the radiator+

CarefulA hot radiator under the window can overdry the bristles. Put them on a table instead.

A table in a room+

YesA dry room with no direct heat, and everything is dry by morning.

A sunny window+

NoIn summer the handle heats up in direct sun and the glue can come loose.

Pro tips from the job

Express cleanser for dry textures only

When I have clients back to back, I use an express cleanser, but only on eyeshadow brushes. I never clean cream textures with it: it's too much hassle and it dries the bristles. It's easier to simply own more brushes.

Clean gel liner off straight away

Don't let gel liner dry on the brush. It dries the brush out and twists the bristles.

From experience: which brushes to start with

If you're only building your kit, a few good brushes are worth more than a box of cheap ones. We go through choosing the ones that really suit you in my one-on-one makeup courses.

Dos and don'ts

Do

  • Antibacterial bar soap
  • Round in circles, flat in strokes
  • Oil for foundation and lipstick
  • Dry flat on a towel
  • Clean gel liner off straight away

With care

  • Shampoo and conditioner for budget natural brushes
  • Express cleanser on dry textures only
  • Boiling water for fine brushes
  • Laundry soap (drying)

Never

  • A wet brush bristles-up
  • Soaking in the sink
  • Hot water
  • Drying on a radiator or in the sun
  • Swirling a flat brush

Three things to take away

  1. 1

    Glue holds everything together.

    Bristles down, no soaking, no hot water and no radiator. That way a brush lasts for years.

  2. 2

    Wash the way you apply.

    You use a round brush in circles, so wash it in circles. A flat one only in strokes, never swirled.

  3. 3

    A good brush is an investment.

    A quality brush copes with plain soap and needs no pricey care. A cheap one fluffs out whatever you use.

Frequently asked questions

What should I wash makeup brushes with?

+

An antibacterial, deep-cleansing bar soap, such as Protex or Safeguard Deep Clean. A gentle creamy soap isn't enough and laundry soap is drying. Wash cheap brushes that fluff out with soap in a gentler shampoo instead.

Can I wash natural-hair brushes with soap?

+

Good ones, yes: in my experience they cope fine. If they start to stiffen, give them a short conditioner or hair mask treatment. Wash budget natural brushes with shampoo, always followed by a quick conditioner.

How do I get foundation or lipstick out of a dense brush?

+

Dissolve and work out the product with a drop of olive or sunflower oil first, then finish with soap. Use only a little oil and keep it out of the base of the brush.

Why are my brushes shedding?

+

Usually because water got into the glue: a wet brush held bristles-up, soaking, hot water, or drying in the sun or on a radiator. Everything in a brush is held together by glue.

How should I dry makeup brushes?

+

Flat on a towel, spaced out and alternating head-to-toe. Not in a humid bathroom, not above a hot radiator and not in direct sun. They dry overnight.

How do I fix a fluffed-out liner brush?

+

Dip just the bristles, not the plastic part, into boiling water for a moment and they pull back together. Some brushes can melt, though, so it's at your own risk.
